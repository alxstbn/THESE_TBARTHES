Study objective

Routine respiratory microbiological sampling is sometimes performed before extubation in postoperative patients without clinical suspicion of pneumonia.

The primary hypothesis of this study is that routine pre-extubation respiratory sampling has a low microbiological yield and limited clinical utility for the subsequent management of postoperative pneumonia (POP).

The analyses therefore focus on:

  1. the frequency of pre-extubation respiratory sampling;
  2. its microbiological yield;
  3. the concordance between pre-extubation and subsequent POP microbiology;
  4. the potential value of prior microbiological information for empirical antimicrobial therapy;
  5. exploratory microbiological trajectories between pre-extubation sampling and POP.

2. Pre-extubation respiratory sampling practice

2.1 Rationale

The first question is not whether pre-extubation microbiology predicts subsequent POP microbiology, but rather how frequently respiratory sampling was actually performed before extubation and what its microbiological yield was.

A pre-extubation sample is considered:

  • not performed when no sampling procedure is recorded;
  • negative when a sample was performed but no microorganism was isolated;
  • positive when at least one microorganism was isolated.

This analysis quantifies the actual microbiological yield of pre-extubation respiratory sampling.

4. Microbiological concordance among positive pre-extubation samples

Among patients with a positive pre-extubation sample, we assessed whether at least one microorganism isolated before extubation was subsequently recovered from the respiratory sample obtained at POP diagnosis.

This analysis assesses microbiological persistence or overlap and should not, by itself, be interpreted as evidence that pre-extubation sampling was clinically useful.

n_concordant <- sum(preEOT_pos$concordant)
n_discordant <- sum(!preEOT_pos$concordant)

prop_concordant <- 100 * n_concordant / nrow(preEOT_pos)

concordance_ci <- binom.test(
  n_concordant,
  nrow(preEOT_pos)
)$conf.int * 100

Results

Among the 20 patients with positive pre-extubation microbiology, at least one microorganism isolated before extubation was subsequently recovered at POP diagnosis in 13 patients (65%; 95% CI 40.8–84.6%).

No matching microorganism was identified in 7 patients (35%).

Interpretation

Although microbiological overlap was observed in approximately two-thirds of patients with a positive pre-extubation sample, this concerned only a small subset of the overall cohort because pre-extubation cultures were positive in only 20 of 69 performed samples.

Microbiological concordance therefore represents only one component of clinical utility. The subsequent analyses will determine whether this prior information was sufficiently frequent and actionable to influence empirical antimicrobial management.

6. Empirical antibiotic adequacy

The principal measure of clinical utility was whether pre-extubation microbiological information was associated with, or could potentially have contributed to, appropriate empirical antimicrobial therapy at the time of POP diagnosis.

Empirical therapy was considered adequate when all causative microorganisms subsequently identified were susceptible in vitro to at least one component of the initial empirical antimicrobial regimen.

Among the 20 patients with positive pre-extubation microbiology, 22 microorganisms were isolated. Oropharyngeal flora was the most frequent finding, accounting for 10 of 22 pre-extubation isolates.

Using the broad definition of microbiological concordance, at least one matching microorganism was identified at subsequent POP diagnosis in 13 of 20 patients (65.0%).

These 9 patients represented only 7.4% of the overall cohort.

Interpretation

The apparent microbiological concordance between pre-extubation and subsequent POP samples was partly driven by non-specific oropharyngeal flora. When concordance was restricted to identified microorganisms, fewer than half of patients with a positive pre-extubation sample had a matching organism at POP diagnosis.

Given the low initial microbiological yield of pre-extubation sampling, a specific matching microorganism was therefore available before POP diagnosis in only a small minority of the overall cohort.

Potential clinical actionability

Among the 20 patients with positive pre-extubation microbiology, 9 (45.0%) had specific microbiological concordance with the microorganism(s) subsequently recovered at POP diagnosis, after excluding concordance based solely on oropharyngeal flora.

Among these nine patients, empirical antimicrobial therapy was inadequate in 4 patients.

Thus, pre-extubation microbiology could potentially have provided relevant information for empirical treatment selection in only 4/122 patients (3.3%) in the overall cohort, corresponding to 4/69 (5.8%) of patients who underwent pre-extubation respiratory sampling and 4/20 (20.0%) of those with a positive pre-extubation sample.

This estimate represents an upper bound of potential clinical actionability rather than demonstrated clinical benefit, because the availability of pre-extubation microbiological identification and susceptibility results at the time of POP treatment initiation could not be established from the available data.

16. Final analytical summary

Pre-extubation respiratory sampling was performed in 69/122 (56.6%) patients, but microbiological yield was only 20/69 (29.0%).

After excluding concordance based solely on oropharyngeal flora, specific microbiological concordance was observed in 9/20 (45.0%) positive samples.

Only 4/122 (3.3%; 95% CI 0.9–8.2%) of the overall cohort had both specific pre-extubation microbiological concordance and inadequate empirical antimicrobial therapy.

This represents an upper-bound estimate of potential clinical actionability rather than demonstrated benefit.

Reported antimicrobial resistance was associated with empirical antimicrobial inadequacy, which occurred in 13/29 (44.8%) patients with resistance compared with 10/70 (14.3%) without reported resistance (OR 4.78, 95% CI 1.61–14.79; P = 0.00311).

Overall, these findings suggest limited potential clinical utility of routine pre-extubation respiratory microbiological sampling for guiding subsequent empirical antimicrobial treatment of POP.
